When does a genomic model build a circuit at all?

1The one idea, in a sentence

A foundation model builds a genuine relational circuit — a computation that connects two distant positions — only when no shortcut can do the job. If a statistic the model can pool over its context window (a bag of local motif counts) is already a sufficient statistic for the target, the model uses that and no circuit is built. The threshold is imperfection, not weakness: a shortcut that is 96.5% accurate does not get displaced by a real circuit unless the task actually demands the last few percent.

Why this reframes the field Interpretability of genomic models has assumed the circuits are there and treated tracing as the hard part. Our results invert that: existence is the open problem, and the usual answer is "no circuit." The models mostly pool, because pooling suffices — which is the mechanistic version of shortcut learning.[shortcut]

2The biology: reading frame and codons

Our first test computation is reading frame, because the ground truth is exact and the biology is textbook. A protein-coding gene is read three letters at a time; each triplet is a codon that names one amino acid. Reading starts at a ATG start codon and proceeds in steps of three. So the "phase" of any nucleotide — is it the 1st, 2nd, or 3rd letter of its codon — is just its distance from the start, counted modulo 3.

The catch — and the whole plot twist of the project — is that a shortcut is hiding in the genetic code itself. Because coding sequence almost never contains a stop codon in the true frame, a model can often guess the frame just by scanning for the frame that has no stops, without ever counting from the start. That is exactly the kind of pooled shortcut §1 warns about, and it is why real genomic models mostly don't build the counter.

3The mechanistic-interpretability toolbox

Mechanistic interpretability (mech-interp) tries to explain a network in terms of the algorithm it runs, not just which inputs correlate with which outputs. Four tools matter for this project.

3.1  Attention heads and induction

A transformer moves information between positions using attention heads. The best-understood relational head is the induction head: it looks back for a previous copy of the current token and predicts what followed it last time — the mechanism behind in-context learning.[induction] Our second circuit (§6) is a genomic member of this family.

3.2  Transcoders and the idea of an "edge"

In a real network the MLP layers are dense — everything mixes into everything — so there is no clean "wire" from one computation to another. A transcoder re-expresses each MLP as a sum of a few sparse, interpretable features; in that replacement model you can draw an edge: one feature's additive contribution to another.[transcoder] Chaining these edges gives an attribution graph — the circuit diagram.[attribution] The price is a faithfulness gap: the tidy replacement is not exactly the model, so every edge must ultimately be checked against the real network.

3.3  Integrated gradients, for saturated components

A confident attention head has a saturated softmax: nudging its score changes nothing, so the naive gradient reads essentially zero (3.7e-15 in our case) exactly where the head is most important. Integrated gradients fixes this by integrating the gradient along a path from a neutral baseline to the real input, recovering the effect the point gradient misses.[IG] We use a QK (query–key) integrated-gradient variant to attribute which token an attention head is keying on.

3.4  Causal patching — the arbiter

The tools above rank components; they do not prove causation. The decisive test is intervention: ablate or replace a component and measure the change in loss. As §4 shows, this is not optional — attribution can rank a completely inert component above the essential one.

The one-line rule of the whole method Attribution ranks; only intervention decides. And a corollary we learned the hard way: a saturated measurement reports nothing exactly where the effect is largest — true for softmax gradients, for variance on sparse targets, and for accuracy pinned at 100%.

4How we actually find a circuit

The workflow is deliberately paranoid, because behaviourally identical models can be mechanistically opposite. Four steps.

Step 1 — the planted twins

We hand-build two tiny models with identical output behaviour (accuracy 0.586 vs 0.583, |Δ| = 0.0034) but opposite internals: one truly computes (position − start) mod 3; the other is a bag of local motif detectors with no frame variable at all. A positive control alone can't falsify a method — twins can, because a good tool must tell them apart.

Step 2 — the forcing task (grokking on demand)

On real DNA the frame counter is worth only ~0.005 nats, 0.40% of the loss, so the model never builds it. We remove the shortcut by training on uniform random codons (no stop-codon signal, no codon bias) with an amino-acid readout that requires the frame. Now the circuit is worth everything — and the model groks it: flat on the guessing ceiling for 2,750 steps, then solves it in ~500 more, phase decodability jumping from 0.41 to 1.0000 as it builds across layers.

Step 3 — cue conflict (why ablation is not enough)

Simply deleting the start codon fails to reveal the circuit: the model just falls back on the pooled route, so ablation reported "no circuit" when one was there. The fix is a cue conflict — insert one nucleotide after the ATG so the anchor says "phase X" while local statistics say "phase X+1", and see which the model follows. On the forcing-task model the far-field result is unambiguous: 1.0000 follows the anchor, 0.0000 follows local content.

Step 4 — the inert-head lesson

The trained model grew two heads that both look like perfect start-codon detectors. Only one is real.

L1H3230×+8.36essential

L1H2 looks like the better detector and the naive gradient ranks it above the essential head — yet it is four million times less causal. It is a naturally-occurring copy of a paranoid control we had invented and worried was contrived. This is the single most important methodological result: only the loss-based intervention got it right.

5The "ceiling" — what it is, and is it significant?

The pooled ceiling is the answer to a precise question: if the model could only pool statistics over its context window and had no relational circuit, how accurate could it possibly be? It is the top of what the shortcut can reach. The whole criterion in §1 is a comparison between the task's demand and this ceiling, so getting it right is load-bearing.

We estimate it by building the best pooled predictor we can and measuring its accuracy on held-out data — for reading frame, a codon-composition estimator pooled over the window. On real hg38 coding sequence that ceiling is 0.9509 at a 240-nt window. Several distinct pooled estimators exist and they must not be confused:

pooled estimator (real hg38 CDS)frame accuracy
±15 nt naive-Bayes bag of motifs0.6419
trained GFM-nano actually achieves0.7936
anchor-free stop-codon scan, 240 nt0.8242

codon-composition pooled ceiling, 240 nt0.9509

The trained model lands at 0.7936 — right at the anchor-free scan ceiling and no higher. It extracts nearly all the frame signal the code leaves lying around, then stops exactly where an anchor would be required. That is the criterion working: the demand (ordinary language-model loss) never rises above what pooling delivers, so the counter is never built.

Is the ceiling statistically significant?

Two different senses of "significant", and both matter:

(a) Is the model's shortfall from a real circuit meaningful, or noise? Yes, and it is enormous, not marginal. The relevant contrast is information, measured in nats. Perfect phase knowledge buys only 0.0241 nats of the per-token loss on real DNA (H(nt) = 1.3714 vs H(nt | phase) = 1.3473), and an anchored counter is worth at most ≤0.005 nats — 0.40% of a 1.27-nat loss. So the model isn't failing to build the circuit; there is essentially no gradient pulling toward it. The gap is not a rounding error — it is a structural fact about the objective.

(b) Is the ceiling number itself a trustworthy bound? This is where we are deliberately conservative. Our estimators are lower bounds on the true shortcut — a more capable pooled model can only push the ceiling up. That direction cuts against our thesis (a higher ceiling makes circuits rarer), so a lower-bound estimate is the honest, self-penalising choice. Crucially, we have now seen this bite twice: two cells whose ceilings we predicted from were beaten —

cellpredicted ceilingmodel reached
B (matched-difficulty detector)0.9260.9735
C (real CDS, no upstream ATG)0.95090.9997

— which is why every ceiling must be re-measured with a model-class-matched estimator (a receptive-field-limited CNN) before it is trusted, and why we report ceilings as bounds, not point predictions. On the significance-testing side, the plan calls for ≥3 seeds per arm reported as an interval and a pre-registered power analysis before the objective-threshold μ\* is claimed, so the threshold comes with error bars rather than a single lucky run.

The honest summary of the ceiling The ceiling is a measured, conservative lower bound on what a shortcut can do, and the model's decision to sit at it is backed by an information-theoretic argument (a circuit is worth <0.5% of the loss), not by a marginal p-value. The remaining statistical work is putting seeds-and-intervals around the threshold, not around the existence of the effect.

6What we found — two circuits, opposite sides of the line

6.1  Reading frame is a spectrum, not a switch settled

The decisive experiment crosses two knobs: is a shortcut available, and is the start-codon detector easy or hard? It did better than confirm "circuit vs no circuit" — it resolved three mechanisms, read off a two-dimensional causal signature (far-field start-codon-ablation cost × the share of frame that follows the anchor under conflict).

6.2  A shortcut-proof task grows a circuit unbidden settled

The mirror-image test: an in-context repeat-copy task where the model must copy the next base from an earlier occurrence of a random DNA unit, after matching a 14-nt prefix. Because the unit is random and the prefix is longer than any local window, no pooled shortcut exists — and here a genuine induction-family circuit forms with no forcing objective at all:

measurementvalue
base-rate copy ceiling (the shortcut)0.2516
copy accuracy, intact0.9608
cue conflict — follows distal edited source0.9635
cue conflict — follows local continuation0.0130

This is the positive counterpart to the reading-frame negative: where a shortcut is truly unavailable, the circuit appears without coercion — exactly what the criterion predicts. Together the two circuits demonstrate the law in both directions.

6.3  What's next next

Three near-term items: trace the relay (the novel object — is it a periodic local re-sync seeded by the anchor?); measure the objective threshold μ\* with seeds and matched-loss comparison; and close the one remaining Gate-0 hole — the LayerNorm faithfulness gap open, since every tracer error rate so far came from LayerNorm-free planted models and both circuits live in a model that has it. Then the climb to biological targets where composition can be held fixed while the label moves: exon definition, the NMD junction rule, and CTCF orientation.
